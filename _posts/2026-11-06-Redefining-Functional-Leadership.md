Most functional leaders I talk to describe their AI plan as giving their people better tools. Procurement gets a contract summarizer, HR gets a drafting assistant, and finance gets faster reconciliation. Each of those helps, and none of them changes what the function is. A department built on the assumption that reading, drafting, matching, and routing are expensive human tasks does not become a different department because those tasks got cheaper.

I call the result the AI-assisted department, and I think it is the most comfortable place for a leader to stop. The org chart survives, the headcount conversation gets deferred, and the quarterly update can report adoption numbers. Meanwhile the structure underneath keeps producing the same queues, handoffs, and approval layers it was built to produce. The organization pays for new capability and keeps the old design, so it collects a fraction of what it paid for.

In the Army, a staff section built around couriers and typed orders did not improve much when it received radios. It improved when someone redesigned the section around what instant communication made possible, including who needed to be in the room and which steps could disappear.

The analogy has a limit worth naming. A radio moved information faster and left every decision with a human, while AI now performs some of the work itself. The redesign therefore has to answer a harder question than where to put the radios. It has to answer what the function is for once much of its current effort stops being scarce.

## Assistance Keeps the Old Design

Every functional design encodes a constraint, and the constraint is almost always the cost of skilled labor. Approval layers exist because reviewing everything was too expensive, so someone set a threshold and sampled below it. Queues exist because a small team could only handle so many requests at once. Monthly cycles and intake forms trace to the same source.

When AI makes each step faster, the instinct is to keep every step and shorten it. A purchase request still enters a queue, still waits for a buyer, still gets routed for approval, and now each stop takes ten minutes instead of two days. That is a real improvement, and it leaves intact the design that created the waiting. The better question is why the request needs a queue at all when a system can check it against policy and vendor history at the moment someone submits it.

Adoption metrics make the trap harder to see. A leader who reports how many people logged into the new tool is measuring activity inside the old design, and the number will rise whether or not the function changes at all. I made a similar point about the hours metric in [Output to Outcome](https://peter.zaffina.net/blog/Output-to-Outcomes/), and the same logic applies here. The measure that matters is what the function delivers and how much of its effort that delivery now requires.

## What Each Function Becomes

Procurement shows the shift clearly. If AI can read every contract, flag every deviation from standard terms, and monitor every vendor continuously, the buyer's job moves from processing requests to negotiating strategy and managing supplier risk. Routine purchases clear automatically against rules the function writes once and revisits on a schedule. The people who remain are the ones who understand a supplier's incentives and can sit across the table from them, and that role needs fewer hands and carries more consequence.

Consider a renewal on a software contract that auto-extends in ninety days. In the old design, someone notices the date, opens a ticket, pulls usage data by hand, and asks the business whether it still needs the product. In the redesigned function, the system flags the renewal six months out, compares usage against the price paid, and puts a recommendation in front of a buyer who already has the negotiating position drafted. The buyer's day is spent on the conversation with the vendor, which is the only part of the process that was ever hard.

HR changes in a similar direction. When benefits questions and policy interpretation are handled by a system that answers at any hour, HR stops functioning as a help desk for the workforce. What remains depends on trust: a manager preparing for a difficult performance conversation, or a reorganization that has to be explained honestly. A redesigned HR function is built around those conversations and measured by them, not by ticket closure.

Finance carries a calendar built around reconciliation, variance analysis, and first-draft reporting. When those happen continuously, the close stops being an event and the function can spend its time advising on the decisions the numbers support. The leader who redesigns around that has to decide who owns the judgment calls the system surfaces, because the volume of flagged items will exceed anything the old team ever reviewed.

IT changes in a way that is harder to see. It stops being the group that builds and supports applications and becomes the group that governs which systems may act on the organization's behalf, with what data, and under whose authority. That is an accountability role, and it sits closer to risk than to operations. The function also has to take a seat in decisions about what the organization allows machines to do, which means IT leaders need to be fluent in the business consequences and not only the technical ones.

Legal moves from reviewing documents one at a time to setting the rules under which automated review operates. The general counsel decides what the system may approve without a lawyer, what must escalate, and what the organization will never delegate. Those thresholds are legal judgments, and they need to be written before the first contract flows through, not after the first dispute.

## The Work That Falls to the Leader

Redesign starts with a plain inventory of what the function does, written as decisions rather than tasks. Reviewing an invoice is a task. Deciding whether to pay it or challenge it is a decision. AI handles tasks well and decisions unevenly, so the sort between the two defines the new shape of the function.

Once the sort exists, the leader has to decide which decisions the system makes alone, which it prepares for a person, and which stay entirely human. Each of those lines carries risk, and the leader owns where it sits. I have argued before that [agents amplify whatever management practice they are placed into](https://peter.zaffina.net/blog/Agents-Amplify-Management/), and the point holds here. A function that cannot say how work is assigned, reviewed, and closed today will automate its confusion at higher speed.

Roles, controls, and metrics then need rewriting together. A job description that lists tasks the system now performs sets people up to defend the wrong things. Controls built to compensate for human error or limited review capacity may no longer be needed, and others will be needed that never existed. Metrics based on volume processed stop describing anything useful, so the function needs measures tied to outcomes, such as cycle time from request to decision and the rate at which automated decisions get overturned.

## Where to Start

Leaders who try to redesign every function at once end up redesigning none of them. Pick one function with high volume, clear rules, and a measurable cost of delay, and redesign it end to end. Procurement intake and accounts payable both fit that description in most organizations, and both produce results that executives can see within a quarter.

Run the old process and the new one in parallel long enough to compare outcomes on real work. The comparison gives you evidence for the council, and it gives the team a chance to find the failures while the old process still catches them. When the new design holds, retire the old one completely. A function that keeps both indefinitely pays for two systems and trusts neither.

Then write down what the function now does and what it no longer does. A short charter that names the decisions the system makes, the decisions people make, and the person accountable for each gives every other function a template to follow. It also gives the next leader in that seat something to inherit besides a pile of tools.

## Accountability Does Not Redesign Itself

A redesigned function moves decisions into systems, and accountability for those decisions stays with named people. Every automated decision needs an owner who can explain it to a council, an auditor, or a regulator in plain terms. In gaming and government environments, someone will ask, and the answer cannot be that the system did it.

That requirement shapes the design. Thresholds need to be explicit, samples of automated decisions need to be reviewed by a person on a regular schedule, and the authority to shut a process off needs to sit with someone who can use it without a committee. I covered the questions a leader should be able to answer before approving any AI tool in [a recent post on vendor approval](https://peter.zaffina.net/blog/Three-Questions/), and the same discipline applies inside the function once the tool is running.

## The Hard Part Is the People

Redesign changes what people do, and a leader who does not say so plainly will lose trust. Some roles shrink. Many become more demanding, because the routine work that used to carry people through a day is gone and what remains is judgment.

That creates a development problem I wrote about in [AI Is Absorbing the Work That Used to Build Judgment](https://peter.zaffina.net/blog/Shared-Services-AI-Strategy/). Junior staff learn by doing the tasks the system now handles, so the redesign has to include a deliberate way to build judgment that no longer depends on those tasks. A function that skips this step will have a sharp redesign on paper and a thin bench within three years.

Leaders also need to be honest with themselves about what they are protecting. Some of the current structure exists because of a constraint that no longer applies, and some exists because the structure gives people status and a defined place. The first kind is easy to retire. The second kind takes courage, and it is the reason many redesigns stall at the AI-assisted stage.

## Lean Organizations Have the Most to Gain

Lean functions carrying heavy compliance and accountability obligations feel this most. Tribal enterprises fit that description: small teams in finance, HR, procurement, legal, and IT that answer to a council, a gaming commission, and federal regulators at the same time. Every hour freed from routine processing can go toward work that protects the nation's assets and sovereignty, which gives the redesign a return that goes beyond efficiency. A function left as it is keeps a small team busy with tasks a machine could already handle.

Redesign is uncomfortable for the person leading it, because it requires admitting that parts of the current structure exist because of old constraints. The leaders who do this work will rewrite job descriptions and move their people into roles built on judgment, then tell their councils plainly what the function now delivers. The ones who wait will find that a vendor has drawn the new boundaries for them.
