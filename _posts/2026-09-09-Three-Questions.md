Every tribal enterprise I talk to is getting pitched an AI product right now, and almost none have a way to decide whether to say yes. The vendor conversation covers features and price. The conversation that actually protects the organization, who owns the risk once the contract is signed, rarely happens until the tool is already running.

I have watched this failure pattern play out in every complex organization I have worked in, from the military to enterprise technology programs. The environment changes. The mistake does not. Someone approves a capability without pinning down who is responsible for it when it breaks, what it costs to walk away from it, and what judgment it quietly replaces. Skip those questions, and you are inheriting a decision after it has already gone wrong, not making one.

For tribal governments, gaming enterprises, and health facilities, the exposure is worse than in most industries. You already carry sovereignty, compliance, and governance across council, commission, and enterprise lines at once. A souring vendor relationship does not stay within one department. It becomes a problem for whoever explains it to the council.

Here is a shorter test than most governance frameworks offer. Before an AI tool gets approved, get a straight answer to three questions.

## Who Owns the Failure?

Every AI system will be wrong at some point. That is not the risk worth debating. The risk worth debating is whether a specific person is on the hook when it happens, and whether that person can shut the system off without asking permission. If nobody can name that person, the tool is not ready for approval.

## What Does It Cost to Leave?

The price on the vendor's invoice is the cost you can see. The real cost shows up the day the vendor changes terms, gets acquired, or stops fitting. Ask what data comes back, in what format, and how long the exit takes, before you sign anything. A vague answer is not a gap in the paperwork. It is the actual price of the tool.

## What Decision Is This Replacing?

AI products get sold as new capability. The more accurate description is that they replace a decision a person used to make, and change who makes it. Name that decision before you approve the tool. If you cannot say what judgment is being displaced, you cannot weigh what you are giving up for what you are gaining.

These are not IT questions. They belong to the people who already answer for gaming compliance, financial controls, and tribal sovereignty, because they will answer for this too. Organizations often treat AI adoption as procurement simply because that is where the conversation starts. It should end with the accountable leaders confirming they can answer all three before anyone signs.

If you can answer them, approve the tool. If not, the gap is not in the technology. It is in your own readiness to govern it, and that is a leadership problem first.
